When this helps

On holiday in Thailand, Japan or Italy, a menu written only in the local language turns every meal into a guess. Many restaurants publish their menu as a PDF on their website or on Google Maps. Download it, translate it before you go, and arrive already knowing what you want, what is spicy and what contains nuts.

It works the other way too: restaurant and café owners can make an English, Hebrew, Russian or Arabic version of their menu in the same design, without copying every dish into a translation site by hand.

What the translated menu looks like

  • Dish names are written out in the new language next to the original name, so you can read them and still show the waiter the name they know.
  • Descriptions are translated naturally, as a sentence about the dish rather than word by word.
  • Prices and marks such as (V), (N) or (P), service charges and currencies stay exactly as in the original.
  • The design is kept: translated text goes where the original text was, in the same colours and sizes, and photos and logos don't move.

Photographed or scanned menus

The translator needs a PDF with real text. If you only have a photo of the menu, turn it into a PDF with JPG to PDF, then run it through OCR to recognise the text. A straight, sharp photo without reflections gives the best result.

A word on accuracy

The translation is done by a language model, which is good at dishes, ingredients and cooking methods. Still, if you have a serious allergy, don't rely on the translation alone: ask the staff. And if you are printing a new menu for your restaurant, have someone fluent in the language read it first.
