Made for life in Israel in English

New immigrants, students, workers from abroad and anyone with family in Israel run into the same thing: important paperwork arrives in Hebrew. These are the documents people translate most:

  • Letters from Bituach Leumi, the tax authority or the municipality: what they're asking for, by when, and how much.
  • Rental contracts: rent, deposit and guarantees, how long the lease runs, and what happens if you leave early.
  • Payslips: which line is tax, which is pension and which is keren hishtalmut.
  • Notices from school or kindergarten: dates, forms to sign and things to bring.
  • Bills and receipts: arnona, electricity, water, and bank or credit card statements.

Read it side by side

With the original layout kept, the English version looks like the Hebrew one: same headings, same table, same place on the page. That makes it easy to find the line you need in the original, for example to point to it when you call the office, or to fill in a Hebrew form.

Understanding vs. official use

Machine translation is for understanding a document and deciding what to do. Anything you submit to an authority, a court, a university or an embassy normally needs a certified translator, sometimes with a notary's approval. More on that in translating an official document.

What to double-check

  • Amounts and deadlines: compare them with the Hebrew original before you act on them.
  • Israeli terms: arnona, keren hishtalmut, mas hachnasa. Many institutions use their own English names on their websites; look them up when it matters.
  • Dates: Israeli documents write day/month/year, so 03/04/2027 is 3 April.
